## The Council of the Gods — Zeus' Decree

Book 5 opens with a second assembly of the gods on Olympus. Poseidon has returned at last from the far-off land of the Ethiopians, where he has been feasting and receiving burnt offerings. The gods assemble again before Zeus, and this time it is Athena who speaks first, rehearsing the full catalog of Odysseus' sufferings: trapped on Calypso's island, his son Telemachus now at sea searching for news of him and in danger of the suitors' ambush. Will no one have pity on this man?

Zeus responds decisively. Enough is enough. He dispatches Hermes, the messenger god with the winged sandals, at once to the island of Ogygia, there to deliver a command to Calypso in no uncertain terms: release Odysseus. No more delay. The time is come. The decree of Zeus is irrevocable. The ship of Odysseus' nostos — long delayed, often wrecked, perpetually drifting — has at last been given the signal to depart.

## Hermes on Ogygia

Homer gives us, in Hermes' journey to Ogygia, one of the most beautiful passages in the Odyssey. The island of Calypso is a garden paradise. A great fire burns in the hearth, filling the island with the scent of cedar and sandalwood. Around the goddess' cave grow wild vineyards heavy with grapes; four fountains of silvery water flow in opposite directions; meadows burst with parsley and violet. Even the immortal gods, Homer tells us, would have gazed upon it in wonder.

Yet this garden of delight is a prison. Hermes finds Calypso within, singing with a sweet voice as she weaves at her loom with a golden shuttle. Outside, on the shore, he finds Odysseus — weeping. As he does every day. As he has done every day for seven years. "He had no delight," Homer says, "no delight in living." He sits upon the rocks, his eyes perpetually wet with tears, his life wasting away in longing for home. For all the beauty of Calypso's island, it is not Ithaca. For all the promise of eternal youth with an immortal goddess, it is not Penelope.

## Calypso's Bargain — Immortality or Home

When Hermes delivers Zeus' command, Calypso's first reaction is bitter, righteous anger. She denounces the double standard of the male gods, who take mortal lovers at will and turn jealous whenever a goddess dares to do the same. The speech is unforgettable: one of the rare moments in Homer where the moral order of the Olympian world is questioned from within, by an immortal being who has herself felt its injustice.

Yet she cannot defy Zeus. She relents. But before she lets Odysseus go, she offers him one last, terrible test: immortality. Stay with me, she says, and you shall be a god forever. You shall never die. You shall never grow old. Remain here with me, and forget your wife and your son and your island home.

Odysseus' answer is the moral core of the Odyssey. He rejects immortality. He chooses mortality, and Ithaca, and Penelope. Even knowing that he will suffer shipwreck, that Poseidon will hunt him across every sea, that he faces years more of wandering before he sees his home — even knowing all this, he chooses the mortal life, the human life, the short, hard life of a man on his own piece of earth. The choice has resonated for three thousand years. To be human, the Odyssey says, is to choose home over paradise, to choose death over a life without end.

## The Raft — Building and Departure

Calypso gives Odysseus tools and materials. Over four days he builds a great raft, hewing the beams with a bronze axe, fitting them together with pegs and joints. On the fifth day Calypso loads it with provisions — wine, bread, water, skins, cloths for a sail — and sends him off with a gentle, following wind. For seventeen days Odysseus sails alone over the open sea. On the eighteenth day, at dawn, Scheria — the land of the Phaeacians — rises on the horizon, a dark smudge in the mist. He is almost there.

## Poseidon's Wrath and the Wreck of the Raft

Poseidon sees him. Poseidon, the earthshaker, the god of the sea, who has hated Odysseus since the day he blinded the Cyclops, sees him sailing peacefully toward land, and his anger rises. He cracks his whip. The sky darkens. The winds rise from every corner, one after another, tearing at the sails, shattering the mast. The waves rise as high as mountaintops. The raft is torn apart. Odysseus is plunged into the sea.

For two days and two nights he drifts in the chaos of the waves, battered, drowning, clinging to a single plank of his shattered raft. When land at last comes within reach, a great wave hurls him against the rocks of the shore, flaying the skin from his hands and knees. He is nearly broken. Only the intervention of Ino — the kind minor sea-goddess, once the mortal Kadmeian princess, who gives him her immortal veil as a talisman — and Athena, who stills the waves and guides him toward the mouth of a gentle river, brings him at last to the safety of the Phaeacian shore.

## The Ending of the Book — Naked and Alone

Book 5 closes with one of the most haunting images in the Odyssey. Odysseus crawls out of the river. He is naked. He is covered in salt and brine. His skin is torn and bleeding. He has nothing — no raft, no crew, no food, no clothes, no name. He has been reduced to the bare fact of a living human body. He staggers into the woods, finds a bed of leaves beneath two olive trees that have grown together from a single root, and falls asleep. Exhausted. Defeated. But alive.

He has reached Scheria. The next step of his journey is about to begin.

## Why Book 5 Matters

Book 5 is the turning point of the Odyssey. Everything before it — the four books of the Telemachy, the councils of the gods, the scattered rumors of Odysseus' existence — has been preparation. Book 5 is where we meet Odysseus himself for the first time. And what a meeting it is: not the heroic king of tradition, not the clever strategist of the Trojan Horse, but a man weeping on a beach, a man trapped between paradise and home, a man who chooses mortality and pays immediately for his choice with shipwreck and near-death.

The book also establishes the pattern that will hold for the rest of the poem: for every blessing granted by Athena and Zeus, there is a corresponding trial sent by Poseidon. Odysseus is the favorite of one god and the hated enemy of another. He is, as Homer will tell us again and again, the man who suffers. But he is also the man who endures. Book 5 gives us the first proof: stripped of everything, he holds on. He refuses to die. He chooses to live — and to go home.
