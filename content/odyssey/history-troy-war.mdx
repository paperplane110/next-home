## Troy as Hisarlık: Schliemann's Dream

For most of modern history, Troy was a myth. Classical travelers visited the Troad — the northwest corner of Anatolia where the legends placed the city — but no one could say with certainty where Priam's citadel had actually stood, or whether it had existed at all. Enter Heinrich Schliemann, a German businessman and amateur archaeologist whose obsession with the Homeric poems drove him to dig at the site of Hisarlık, a large artificial mound (or tell) overlooking the Hellespont, in the early 1870s.

Schliemann was a controversial figure. He was not a trained archaeologist by the standards of his day, and his methods were destructive: he dug a massive trench straight through the tell, destroying many layers in his haste to reach what he believed was Homer's Troy. But he was right about the location. Hisarlık, as subsequent excavations would confirm, is indeed the site of the city that the Greeks called Troy and the Hittites called Wilusa. The question was which of its many destroyed layers was the Troy of the war.

## The Nine Cities of Troy

One mound, nine cities. The tell at Hisarlık contains the superimposed remains of nine successive settlements, built one on top of the other over roughly four thousand years. Archaeologists have numbered them Troy I through Troy IX, from the earliest (c. 3000 BCE) to the latest (the Roman city of Ilium Novum, abandoned in the Byzantine period).

The candidates for "Homeric" Troy are Troy VI and Troy VIIa. Troy VI (c. 1750–1300 BCE) was a magnificent fortified city with massive sloping walls, towers, and a royal citadel — exactly the kind of city that might have inspired legends of great walls built by Poseidon and Apollo. But Troy VI appears to have been destroyed by an earthquake, not by human attackers: the walls are tilted and fractured in a way consistent with seismic activity.

Troy VIIa (c. 1300–1180 BCE) is the layer that followed. It was a poorer, more crowded city than Troy VI — the houses were smaller, more closely packed, and storage jars were dug into the floors, as if the inhabitants were expecting a siege. And Troy VIIa was destroyed by fire. The archaeological record shows massive burning, and some arrowheads and sling stones have been found in the destruction debris. This is the layer that most archaeologists today identify with the sack of Troy — or, rather, with the historical event or events that underlie the legend.

## The Ahhiyawa Question

If Troy was Wilusa to the Hittites, then who were the Achaeans? Hittite texts from the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE refer repeatedly to a western power called Ahhiyawa — a name that linguistically matches the Homeric Achaioi, the term Homer uses for the Greeks at Troy. The Hittite texts describe Ahhiyawa as a kingdom across the sea, sometimes hostile, sometimes allied, whose rulers were treated by the Hittite great king as equals.

The Ahhiyawa texts are fragmentary, and their interpretation remains controversial. But the picture they paint is suggestive: a Mycenaean kingdom (or coalition of kingdoms) with military and naval interests in western Anatolia, in diplomatic contact — and sometimes in conflict — with the Hittite empire and with local rulers like the king of Wilusa. One text, the Tawagalawa Letter, mentions a dispute over Wilusa that had been resolved. Another records Hittite military operations against Ahhiyawan-backed rebels. This is not the Trojan War of Homer. But it is evidence that Mycenaean Greeks were active in the Troad in the right period, and that conflicts over Troy did occur.

## Ten Years, or Many Raids?

The tradition of a ten-year siege is almost certainly a poetic invention, or a conflation. In the Late Bronze Age, military campaigns of even a single season were logistically demanding. A sustained siege of ten years would have been unthinkable without the infrastructure of a modern state. But what is plausible is a series of Mycenaean raids on western Anatolia over a period of years or decades — attacks on coastal towns and trade routes, of which a raid on Troy was simply the largest or most famous.

Homer's ten-year war may be the poetic condensation of these many separate events into a single, unified narrative. The poet himself hints at this: the Catalogue of Ships lists not only Troy but many nearby towns that the Achaeans sacked in the course of the war. The historical reality, then, was probably something like this: around 1200 BCE, as the Late Bronze Age order was beginning to unravel, a Mycenaean expedition (or coalition) attacked and destroyed the city of Troy VIIa. Over the following centuries, that raid was remembered, elaborated, merged with other traditions, and transformed — by the genius of oral poetry — into the ten-year war of the Iliad.

## Homer's Poetic Expansion

In the end, the question "Was there a real Trojan War?" admits of no simple yes-or-no answer. There was almost certainly a historical Trojan War — or, more accurately, a series of Trojan wars — of which Homer's war is the mythic shadow. The archaeology confirms that Troy was a real city, that it was destroyed violently around the right time, and that Mycenaean Greeks were active in the region. The Hittite texts confirm that the political situation was exactly the kind that might produce such a conflict.

But the Iliad is not history. It is a poem. Its heroes are not historical figures in any sense we would recognize. Its war is a symbolic landscape, not a reconstruction of a Bronze Age military campaign. The miracle of the poem is that it took the scattered memories of a real event, preserved through centuries of oral tradition, and made of them something far more enduring than any empire: a story that would still be read, three thousand years later, on the far side of the world.
