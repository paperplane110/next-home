## The First Arrow

The book opens mid-gesture: Odysseus strips off his rags, leaps to the threshold, pours out the arrows at his feet, and speaks for the first time as himself — now for a target no man has ever hit, he says; with Apollo's glory on his side, let me see if I can strike it. The first arrow takes Antinous in the throat as he lifts his wine cup; the blood runs from his nostrils, the cup falls. The suitors think it an accident — no man would shoot in a feast — and rage at the beggar until he answers them.

His answer is the poem's indictment, spoken over the threshold: you dogs, he says, you never thought I would come back from Troy, so you devoured my house and courted my wife while I was alive, fearing neither the gods nor the vengeance of men. Now the cords of death are lashed around you all. The words are the trial the poem has conducted for eight books, delivered as its verdict.

## Eurymachus' Bargain

Eurymachus steps forward with the bargain that has been forming in the suitors' minds since the first arrow: the man who led us into this is dead — Antinous — and dead is the instigator; spare the rest, and we will repay in full, land and herds, bronze and gold, until your heart softens. It is the suitors' economy even at the last: they offer to buy back their lives with the goods they were stealing.

Odysseus' refusal is the poem's fixed point: not if you gave me everything you own and all your fathers' wealth piled on it, he says; not until the last of you pays for his outrage. The poem allows no conversion of blood into goods — xenia, the guest-law they spent the poem breaking, has its accounting, and it is not in cattle. Fight or flee, he tells them: the door is behind you, but you will not reach it. The slaughter begins.

## The Battle in the Hall

The battle is the poem's one set piece of war, fought with tables for shields and the weapons of a feast. Odysseus' arrows take the first rank — Eurymachus dies charging, Amphinomus by Telemachus' spear — and the young man runs for the armor. His one mistake makes the battle: he leaves the storeroom door unbarred, and Melanthius slips through it to arm the suitors, twelve shields, twelve spears, twelve helmets. Odysseus, seeing the armor, knows the traitor, and sends Eumaeus and Philoetius to take him; they catch him in the act and hoist him to the rafters, bound hand and foot. The poem's note on the binding is one of its grimmest images: they leave him strung up for later.

Athena joins the fight as Mentor, and the poem does something strange with its goddess: she lets the suitors' volley fly first, to test the four men in the hall — and when it flies wide, the rout is over. The suitors' spears miss; the four men's do not. One by one the named suitors fall — Eurydamas, Amphimedon, Demoptolemus, Eurynomus and the rest — and the poem says what it has been promising since Book 2: the suitors lay in heaps, like fish on a shore, like oxen under the ax. The feast is over, and the feasters are the meat.

## Mercy for the Bard and the Herald

Two lives come up for judgment before the killing ends, and the poem pauses to sort them. Phemius the bard, still holding his lyre, clasps Odysseus' knees: he sang for the suitors under compulsion, he says — no man forced to sing should die for it; and Telemachus vouches for him. Medon the herald crawls out from under a chair, where he has hidden through the whole battle, and Telemachus vouches for him too. The two are spared and sent outside.

The distinction the poem draws here is the book's hardest point: the bard who sang for the suitors lives, the maids who slept with them will die. What saves the bard is not his innocence but his office — the poem protects the singer, since it is itself a song. What condemns the maids is not sex but the choice of beds, made with the household's enemies while the household's queen wove her delay upstairs. The slaughter has its own jurisprudence, and the poem states it without softening: the herald and the bard go free, the faithless servants do not.

## The Faithless Maids

The aftermath is the poem's most debated cruelty, and it is written in full. The twelve maids who went with the suitors are made to carry out the bodies, scrub the blood from the hall, and then, on Odysseus' order, they are hanged all together, row on row, their feet twitching briefly in the air. Melanthius is cut down and mutilated — nose, ears, hands, the rest — and the poem lets the sentence stand without comment. The house is then cleansed with fire and sulfur, and the loyal maids come down and kiss their master, the poem says, and he knew them all, and the longing for home went through him like a pain.

The book does not ask the reader to approve the hangings; it asks them to be seen. Justice in this poem is proportionate and terrible, and the proportion is the poem's: the maids who fed the enemies of the house feed the house's vengeance with their bodies. The sulfur that cleanses the hall afterwards is the poem's own note that something here needs cleansing.

## Key Themes of Book 22

Book 22 is the poem's verdict, executed. The slaughter has been prepared for eight books — the weapons hidden, the doors barred, the company assembled — and when it comes it is less a battle than an accounting: the suitors die in order, named, each death a line in a ledger the poem has been keeping since Book 2. The book's discriminations are its moral core: the bard and herald spared, the traitor and the faithless maids not, and the difference lies in which side of the house each one served. Revenge in the Odyssey is not rage but restitution — the suitors pay with their bodies for the goods they consumed, and the poem's one mercy, extended to the singer, goes to the office that keeps the whole story alive.
