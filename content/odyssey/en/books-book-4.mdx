## The Wedding Feast at Sparta

Telemachus and Peisistratus drive through the night from Pherae and reach Sparta at daybreak. They arrive in the middle of a double wedding: Menelaus is marrying his daughter Hermione to Neoptolemus, Achilles' son, and his son Megapenthes to a Spartan bride. Eteoneus the herald asks his master whether the strangers should be turned away to another house at such a moment. Menelaus' answer sets the tone of the whole book: no one should be sent away from this palace, least of all after the wanderings he himself endured. Bath, oil, feast — the two boys sit beside the king.

Telemachus whispers to Peisistratus that the hall gleams with bronze and gold and silver and ivory, like the court of Zeus himself. Menelaus overhears the marvel and corrects it gently: no mortal house competes with the gods. But his wealth came at a price, he says — seven years of wandering, Cyprus, Phoenicia, Egypt. The splendor of Sparta is the yield of a nostos that almost failed.

## Helen's Recognition

Helen descends from her high-roofed chamber, spindle in hand, like Artemis — the crimson yarn she weaves shows the battles of Troy. She studies the stranger at once and names him: the resemblance to Odysseus is unmistakable. Here is the son the father left behind, a babe at the breast, when the Achaeans sailed for Troy. The whole hall weeps; Peisistratus mourns his brother Antilochus, killed at Troy, and Menelaus grieves for everyone he left behind on the beach at Troy.

Then Helen mixes a drug into the wine, a herb of Egypt — where the soil breeds poisons and cures alike — that dissolves grief for a day. It is her gift, learned in a land of healers, and the evening becomes bearable. Each spouse then tells one story of Odysseus at Troy. Helen's: he came into the city as a beggar, self-mutilated, and she alone knew him, bathed him, kept his secret — and he slipped back out having killed many Trojans. Menelaus' counter-story: inside the wooden horse, when Helen circled it calling each man's name in his wife's voice, Odysseus clamped the men down, and no one answered.

## The Old Man of the Sea

Next day Menelaus asks Telemachus his errand, hears of the suitors devouring the house, and answers with rage and one of his bloodiest images: it will be like the doe who bedded her fawns in a lion's lair — when the lion returns, the fawns' fate is sealed. Telemachus declines the gift of horses: Ithaca is a goat-pasture, no land for horses. He takes instead a mixing bowl of silver, the finest thing in the palace.

And then, pressed for news of Odysseus, Menelaus tells the story of his own homecoming: twenty days becalmed at Pharos off the Egyptian coast, his crew starving, until Eidothea, daughter of Proteus the Old Man of the Sea, took pity. Her plan: at noon Proteus comes up from the sea to sleep among his seals. Hide under sealskins, count the herd as it passes, then seize him and hold. Proteus fights back the only way a shape-shifter can — lion, serpent, leopard, boar, running water, a flowering tree. Hold him through every shape, Eidothea said, and at last he will tire and tell the truth.

## Proteus' News

They held, and the old god answered. The news ran from the worst: Ajax dead, his boast against Poseidon answered with the trident; Agamemnon ambushed at his homecoming feast by Aegisthus. For Menelaus himself the god had a different sentence: because he is Helen's husband and son-in-law of Zeus, he will not die at Argos — the gods will carry him to Elysium at the ends of the earth, where life is easiest. And finally, the news the poem has withheld since Book 1: Proteus had seen Odysseus — alive, weeping on Calypso's island, his eyes never dry, detained against his will.

So the Telemachy closes its loop of information. Nestor told of the homecomings; Menelaus confirms that the one that matters has not happened yet. The son now knows his father is alive, and he is the only one on Ithaca who does.

## The Plot at Ithaca

The poem cuts back to Ithaca, where the suitors throw the discus by the shore. Noemon, the ship-owner, asks Antinous when the ship he lent Telemachus will be back, since he needs it for a cargo run. The question lands like a thrown stone: the suitors did not know Telemachus had sailed. Antinous rages — the voyage is a great deed that has got away from them. He crews a ship and stations it at Asteris, the rocky islet between Ithaca and Same, to ambush the prince on his way home.

Medon the herald overhears the plot and carries it to Penelope. Her knees give way; she sits speechless in a doorway, then weeps for the double threat — the suitors at her son's throat on land and sea. Eurycleia confesses she knew of the voyage and kept it hidden. Penelope bathes and prays to Athena; and that night Athena sends a phantom in the shape of Iphthime, Penelope's own sister, to stand over her head and promise that Telemachus will come back. When Penelope asks the one question that matters — what of Odysseus — the phantom will not say. Some news the poem still withholds.

## Key Themes of Book 4

Book 4 is the Telemachy's second stop. Book 3 showed the rules of nostos kept: the pious king, the smooth return. Book 4 shows their price and their exceptions. Menelaus' house is the poem's one restored household — Helen home, weaving, telling stories at her husband's table. But the restoration is soaked in grief: seven years of wandering, a brother killed, friends dead at Troy, and the drug that banishes sorrow works for one evening only. Against it stand Agamemnon's house, the marriage destroyed, and the suitors' conspiracy tightening in Ithaca while the boy is away. In Book 4, nostos is not a happy ending but a long convalescence; and the household that keeps it must be guarded in turn.
