## The Entrusted One

Mentor's role in the epic can be stated in one sentence: before Odysseus sailed for Troy, he left his household in the care of this old friend. That makes Mentor the only man on Ithaca outside the family itself who bears a formal responsibility for the house — and while the suitors occupy the palace, that responsibility presses on him alone.

Homer does not spend time on Mentor's own story. His meaning lies in his position: when Athena needs to speak in a mortal's voice, the face she chooses is Mentor's. To borrow his shape is to borrow that entrusted authority.

## Mentor's Rebuke

At the assembly of Book 2, Telemachus asks the people for a ship and men, and no one answers. Mentor rises and delivers the poem's sharpest rebuke of public silence:

> I am not angry with the suitors — they will pay for it with their own heads. I am angry with all of you: you sit in silence, and not one of you rises to stop them.

He turns the charge on the whole of Ithaca: wrongdoing persists not through the wrongdoers but through the silence of bystanders. The speech is the political heart of Book 2, and it fixes what the name "Mentor" means inside the epic — the man who says what no one else dares to say.

## The Goddess's Armor

Athena takes Mentor's shape three times. On the shore in Book 2, she promises Telemachus as Mentor: the ship will be hers to fit out, the crew hers to find, his only job the provisions. In the slaughter of Book 22, she appears again in Mentor's form in the hall, cheering Odysseus on — and then turns into a swallow, perches on a rafter, and watches the vengeance through to the end like a spectator. In the final battle of Book 24, she halts both sides in Mentor's shape and voice and makes the peace hold.

Three appearances, one logic: Athena comes as the entrusted one, because her role in this epic is also that of an entrusted one — charged with bringing Odysseus home and taking Ithaca back from the suitors. The god borrows the man's face; the man carries the god's commission.

## The Legacy of a Name

Mentor's quietest influence lies outside the epic. In 1699 the French bishop Fénelon wrote Les Aventures de Télémaque for the education of a prince, and in it Mentor — Athena's disguise — became the young hero's tutor. The book swept Europe, "Mentor" became synonymous with the wise counselor, and the English word mentor grew from it. An old man who appears only a handful of times in Homer ended by lending his name to every guide for the three hundred years that followed.
