## The Homeric Question

For more than two thousand years, readers of the Iliad and the Odyssey have asked: Who was Homer? The ancient Greeks had no doubt that both epics were the work of a single blind poet, most commonly associated with the Ionian city of Smyrna or the island of Chios. Ancient biographies — the *Vitae Homeri* — circulated from at least the fifth century BCE, filling in the missing details of his life with legend and anecdote. But as modern scholarship developed in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, a series of fierce debates, collectively known as the "Homeric Question," shook the traditional picture.

The Analysts, led by scholars such as Friedrich August Wolf (whose *Prolegomena ad Homerum* of 1795 is the foundational text of modern Homeric studies), argued that the Iliad and the Odyssey were not the work of a single poet but compilations of older lays stitched together by later redactors. They detected contradictions, seams, and interpolations and attributed the poems to multiple hands or centuries of accretion. Opposed to them stood the Unitarians, who insisted on the essential artistic unity of each epic and the single intelligence behind it. The debate raged for more than a century and a half, producing mountains of scholarship but no consensus.

## The Oral-Formulaic Revolution

The breakthrough came in the 1920s and 1930s with the work of the American classicist Milman Parry and his student Albert B. Lord. Parry demonstrated that the distinctive language of the Homeric poems — the repeated epithets, the formulaic phrases, the type-scenes of feasting and arming and departing — was not a sign of multiple authorship but of an oral poetic tradition of immense sophistication. Working first in the Balkans and then with the last living Yugoslavian oral epic singers (the *guslari*), Parry and Lord showed how illiterate bards could compose long narrative poems of thousands of lines before live audiences, drawing on a tradition of inherited formulas that allowed rapid, on-the-spot composition in meter.

The oral-formulaic theory (as it came to be called) transformed the Homeric Question. Neither a single "Homer" nor a committee of redactors was necessary to explain the poems; instead, they were the product of a centuries-old living tradition in which generation after generation of singers inherited, adapted, and recomposed the stories of the Trojan War and Odysseus' return. On this view, the "Homer" of tradition might be understood as the name for one especially great singer — or, perhaps, for the tradition itself.

Today, most scholars accept some version of the oral-formulaic account while continuing to debate how much of each epic's final shape we owe to individual genius, how much to tradition, and how and when the poems were first written down. The current consensus places the composition of the Iliad in the later eighth century BCE and the Odyssey perhaps a generation or two later, though both poems draw on material that may reach back to the Late Bronze Age.
