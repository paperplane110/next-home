## The Bag of Winds

Aeolus, keeper of the winds, receives the fleet on his floating island and plays host for a month. At parting he gives Odysseus a bag stitched from a whole oxhide, all the contrary winds sealed inside, with only the west wind left out to drive them home. Nine days they sail; on the tenth, Ithaca's coast is in sight — fires burning, men on the shore. And then, within sight of home, Odysseus falls asleep at the helm.

The crew has nursed a suspicion through the whole voyage: the bag is treasure, and the captain means to keep it. Their speech is the book's study in self-destruction — ten days from home, and they open the bag to see. The winds burst out, the ship is swept back to Aeolia, and when Odysseus begs a second gift, Aeolus' answer is the poem's theology in one line: a man the blessed gods detest must wander on.

## The Laestrygonian Harbor

Six days of rowing bring them to Telepylus, the Laestrygonians' city, where the harbor is a narrow cleft in the cliffs. Odysseus moors outside; the scouts go in and meet a girl drawing water, and then her mother, a mountain of a woman, and then her father, king Antiphates — who seizes one of the scouts and eats him. The other two run, and the Laestrygonians come down on the harbor like giants throwing boulders: the trapped ships are speared like fish, the crews devoured on the shore. Only Odysseus' ship escapes — he had kept it outside the harbor, the one time in the wanderings when caution and not appetite decides the day. The fleet of twelve is now a single ship.

## Circe's Pigs

The survivors reach Aeaea, Circe's island, daughter of the sun. After rest, Eurylochus leads a scouting party inland and finds her hall: wolves and mountain lions that fawn on visitors, drugged men in animal bodies. Circe invites the scouts to her feast, and the food carries her drug; a touch of her wand turns them into pigs — bristles, snouts, grunts — though their minds, the poem insists, stay human. Eurylochus alone hangs back, and brings the news.

Odysseus goes alone. The poem knows this is the story's crossroads and gives it a god's intervention: Hermes meets him on the path as a young man, hands him moly — black root, milk-white flower, hard for men to dig — and the counter-spell. Drugged wine, drawn sword: when Circe's charm fails and the blade is at her throat, she names him — you must be Odysseus, the man Hermes warned me of — and the witch's hall becomes a host's house.

## A Year on Aeaea

Circe restores the men with a second drug: the pigs become men again, younger, taller, handsomer than before. Then comes the strangest thing in the wanderings — a year passes on the island, and the poem marks it in a single line, as if time itself were drugged. The crew, who have forgotten nothing this time, come to Odysseus and say it plainly: we want to go home. He asks Circe for passage, and she names its price: no one leaves this island without first visiting the house of Hades, to hear the prophet Tiresias. The most famous book of the poem — the descent to the dead — arrives as a travel requirement.

## Elpenor's Fall

The book ends with the death the next book begins from. Elpenor, the youngest of the crew, not much for sense and no man for courage, slept on the roof for the cool air. The noise of departure woke him, and in his hurry he forgot the ladder: he fell headlong from the roof and broke his neck, and his soul went down to the house of Hades. Odysseus does not know yet. The unburied man is waiting for him at the door of the dead, and his burial will be the first duty of the next morning.

## Key Themes of Book 10

Book 10 is the crew's book — the longest, clearest look at the men Odysseus keeps losing. The bag, the harbor, the pig-sty: three disasters, three shapes of the same failure, obedience losing to appetite. Circe's island tests the difference between man and beast, and the poem's answer is precise: the body can be made a pig's while the mind stays human; what makes a man is not his shape but what he remembers and wants. The men who ate the lotus lost the want; the men who became pigs kept it. The wanderings' real monster is not Circe but the crew's own hunger, and Book 11, where the dead speak, is the price of it.
