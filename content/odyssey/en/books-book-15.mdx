## Athena's Warning and the Gifts

Athena appears to Telemachus in Sparta with the poem's most practical divine message: go home now. The suitors' ambush waits at Asteris; the strongest of them, Eurymachus, is pressing his suit with gifts; and Penelope's father and brothers are urging her toward him. Sail through the night, she says, and a god will send the wind.

The leave-taking shows the Telemachy at its harvest. Menelaus gives the mixing bowl of silver — the great work of Hephaestus, he says, a gift of the king of Sidon — and Helen gives a robe she wove herself, the finest thing in her house, to be worn by the bride who is not yet chosen. An eagle flies over carrying a goose snatched from the yard, and Helen reads the omen: Odysseus will come home and take vengeance on the suitors. The boy who left Ithaca three books ago asking whether his father was alive leaves Sparta carrying a prophecy and a queen's needlework.

## The Fugitive Seer

At Pylos, Telemachus asks Peisistratus to drop him at the ship without stopping at the palace — he cannot bear Nestor's hospitality again when home is burning. The tact of it is a grown man's tact. As they ready the ship, a stranger arrives: Theoclymenus, a seer of Argos, a fugitive who killed a man of his own clan and has come to the shore looking for passage. Telemachus takes him aboard. The prince who set out to learn judgment now has a man's life in his hands, and he does the right thing without being told.

Out on the water the seer earns his passage: a hawk, Apollo's bird, tears a dove apart above the ship, and Theoclymenus reads it — no house in Ithaca is more kingly than yours; yours is the ruling line forever. Telemachus prays that it come true. The poem's two visionaries — the seer of signs and the reader of omens — have both bet on this house now.

## Eumaeus Remembers

Back at the hut, the evening runs its course. The beggar offers again to go down to the palace and beg among the suitors — the plan disguised as a kindness — and Eumaeus again refuses: the suitors would crush an old man, and there is no need; wait for Telemachus, he will clothe you. Then the beggar turns the talk to the old king, and Eumaeus tells what the palace never says: Laertes lives in the country like a laborer, and has not eaten at the palace since his son sailed. The old man's grief, he says, is worst in the seasons — he goes down to the vineyard and lies in the soil and mourns.

The swineherd's own childhood follows, the story he told in the last book: a king's son stolen by the Phoenicians, sold to Laertes, raised with Ctimene almost as her brother. The two men sit late into the night, one telling the truth of his life, the other measuring it.

## The Ambush Comes to Nothing

At dawn Telemachus' ship makes the Ithacan coast. He sends the crew to town with the ship, sends Theoclymenus to the house of Piraeus for safety, and goes himself up the mountain path — to the swineherd's hut, as Athena commanded. The ambush at Asteris has waited in vain all night; the ship slipped past in the dark. The suitors' own vessel comes into the harbor, and the poem shows their failure through their own eyes: Amphinomus, watching from the shore, sees the ship and understands — a god has saved the boy. The suitors land, gather in a circle, and Antinous proposes the next murder: if the sea will not take him, kill him in the country, where the fields hold no witness. Amphinomus, the suitor the poem has marked for death with the rest, argues for the omens first. The vote goes his way, but the plot is only postponed.

## Key Themes of Book 15

Book 15 is the poem's great convergence. Three threads that have run in parallel — the boy in Sparta, the beggar on the mountain, the suitors in the palace — are drawn toward one hut on the same night, and the next book will knot them there. The book's tone is the calm before that knot: omen on omen, each one read by a different reader, all pointing the same way. Helen reads the eagle, Theoclymenus the hawk, Athena's plan works in the dark sea; and the poem's own answer to the omens is Eumaeus' talk of the old king lying in his vineyard, mourning his son. The house of Odysseus is a house of readers now — everyone is waiting for the same return, and only the suitors read nothing.
