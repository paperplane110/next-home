## The Goddess Metis and Her Daughter

Mētis (μῆτις) is both a proper name and a common noun in Greek. As a name, Metis is the goddess of wisdom and craft, the first wife of Zeus; as a noun, it means a very specific capacity: resourcefulness in the moment — not book-learning, but the skill of knowing what to do when the plan falls apart.

In Hesiod's Theogony, Zeus swallowed the pregnant Metis on the strength of a prophecy, and Athena was born fully armed from his forehead, carrying all of her mother's intelligence with her. This explains the bond between Athena and Odysseus in the Odyssey: she is not just any patron — she is the incarnation of mētis, and Odysseus is the only mortal in the poem who shares that quality with her. The Homeric epithet *mētieta* (the cunning counselor) belongs in the poem to exactly two beings: Zeus and Odysseus.

## Polymētis: Odysseus of the Many Wiles

Almost every standard epithet of Odysseus points to the mind: polymētis ("of many wiles"), polytropos ("of many turns"), polytlas ("much-enduring"). The poet's first line sings of "the polytropos man" — the epithet is the poem in miniature.

His résumé of mētis runs end to end: Troy, which ten years of siege could not take, fell in one night to his wooden horse; in the Cyclops' cave he did not fight force with force, but escaped with a "gift," a lie, and a sharpened olive stake; back on Ithaca he disguised himself as a beggar and watched his enemies one by one from inside their own hall; and he is a professional liar — to Athena, to the swineherd, to the suitors, to his own wife, he tells one invented "Cretan" autobiography after another. These tales are never padding: each fabrication is a live demonstration of his mētis and a test of the listener's judgment. Odysseus is the poet's double — the Odyssey itself is a vast weave of truth and fiction.

## "No One": A Pun in One Word

The finest moment of mētis is a word game in Book 9. Trapped in Polyphemus' cave, Odysseus gives his name as "No one" (Outis, Οὖτις). When the blinded giant shouts to his neighbors — "No one is killing me!" — they answer: if no one is harming you, then it is Zeus-sent illness; bear it. They scatter, and Odysseus slips free.

The point is the Greek pun: mē tis ("no one") sounds almost exactly like mētis ("cunning"). Asked for his name, Odysseus hands over his essential weapon — his strength lies not in his identity but in the emptiness of it; not in the name, but in its disappearance. The pun is among the most famous clever lies in Western literature: the victory of mētis is the victory of the nameless over the named.

## Mētis against Biē: Wit against Force

In the world of the Odyssey, the villains almost all possess only biē (force): the Cyclopes are "far stronger than the gods" yet entirely without craft, and a single lie undoes them; the suitors have numbers and arrogance, and Odysseus takes all one hundred and eight of them with a bow, a moment, and a disguise.

Homer states the theme outright in a song-within-the-song in Book 8. At the Phaeacian feast, the bard Demodocus sings of Hephaestus, who caught Ares and Aphrodite in adultery with an invisible golden net, and the gods gathered round laughing. The war-god beaten by the craftsman's device: that is the fable of mētis over biē, and a rehearsal of Odysseus' own story. In Book 13, Athena says it most plainly when she meets Odysseus again: among mortal men you are far the best in counsel and in storytelling, while among the gods I am famed for my wisdom and my shifts — we two know how to spin our tricks.

## The Limits of Cunning

Homer does not hide mētis' shadow. In Book 24, Odysseus finds his aged father Laertes and opens not with the truth but with a false identity, making the old man grieve before he is allowed to recognize his son — many readers find the test almost cruel. His testing of Penelope and his concealment from the old nurse likewise treat the people he loves most as objects of strategy. Mētis is an instrument of human nature, and it carries the capacity to wound.

Yet the Odyssey's verdict is clear: in this world, mētis is the virtue of survivors. Cunning is not a stain on virtue but the last weapon of mortals — those with no divine power, no army, and no protection. The modern scholars Detienne and Vernant, in Cunning Intelligence in Greek Culture and Society, made its most systematic defense: mētis is a "practical intelligence" that does not pursue eternal truth but handles the fleeting occasion — and the long denigration of it in the philosophical tradition only throws Homer's esteem for it into relief.
