## The Bow of Iphitus

Penelope goes to the storeroom for the bow, and the poem stops the contest before it starts to tell the weapon's biography. It came from Iphitus, son of Eurytus, the great archer who challenged Apollo himself; the two young men met at Messene, where Odysseus had come to recover sheep stolen from Ithaca, and exchanged gifts of friendship — the bow, the sword, the spear. The frame story darkens the gift: Iphitus went on searching for his stolen mares, and Heracles murdered him, guest in his own house, for the horses' sake. The bow passed to Odysseus, who never took it to Troy.

So the weapon that will decide the poem carries its own small Odyssey inside it: friendship, theft, a guest-killing, an heirloom left at home. The bow has been waiting in the storeroom for its one owner, and the poem gives it a history worthy of the shot it is about to make.

## Twelve Axes and the Rules

Penelope carries the bow into the hall and announces the terms: whoever strings it and sends an arrow through twelve axe-heads — the axes lined up like a ship's keel-timbers — that man takes her from the house. She says the name of the house out loud for the first time in the poem's second half: she is leaving the house of Odysseus. The phrasing is the contest's trap, and the poem lets it stand: the only man who can win it is the man whose house she is leaving.

The announcement reduces the hall to tears in order of loyalty — Eumaeus weeps, Philoetius weeps, and Antinous mocks them for it. Telemachus sets the axes in their trench, straightens them, and tries the bow himself first. Three times he bends it; the fourth, the poem says, he would have strung it — and his father's signal stops him. The prince's near-success is the book's first disclosure: the blood of the house can bend its bow.

## The Suitors Fail

Then the suitors try, in order, and the poem turns the contest into a slow exposure. Leodes the soothsayer, the only suitor who hated their crimes, goes first — his hands are too soft, and he prophesies his own end: this bow will break the heart and spirit of many a lord. Antinous calls for tallow and fire to soften the horn. One by one they fail, down to Eurymachus, the best of them, who holds the bow over the fire, bends it, and cannot. His lament is the poem's judgment, spoken by the judged: it is not the marriage that grieves him, he says — there are other women — but the shame, that we are so much weaker than the man whose wife we court.

The arithmetic of the poem's second half is now written on the wall of the hall: a hundred and eight men, and not one of them can bend the king's bow. The suitors have failed the house's one instrument, and the poem has measured them by it.

## The Oath in the Courtyard

The beggar asks for a turn, and the suitors refuse him — the shame of a beggar succeeding is worse to them than their own failure. Penelope insists, over Eurymachus' objection, that the stranger be allowed to try; and Telemachus, in the first full exercise of his authority, takes the matter out of everyone's hands: the bow is his to give, he says — and he sends his mother upstairs. The poem marks her amazement at the man her son has become, and the plan made in the hut eight books ago moves into its final position.

Outside, Odysseus draws Eumaeus and Philoetius apart and asks the question he has asked in disguise all along: if Odysseus came back, where would you stand? Their answer this time is not a hope but an oath, and he seals it with the proof — the scar, the thing no one knows. Then the orders: Eumaeus, carry me the bow when the time comes, and bar the women in their rooms; Philoetius, bar the courtyard gate. The two herdsmen take their posts. The house closes.

## The Stringing

Eumaeus walks the bow through the furious hall and lays it in the beggar's hands; the suitors' outcry fills the room. Odysseus turns it in the light, end to end, the way a musician examines his instrument — checking the horn for worm, the stringing for rot — and the poem's simile says the rest: as easily as a bard, who knows his craft, strings a new peg on the lyre and twists the sheep-gut chord, so Odysseus strung the great bow. With his right hand he tested the string, and it sang under his touch like a swallow. Zeus thunders, and the king's heart sings back.

The suitors' faces drain. The poem's favorite image — the disguised man revealed by his craft — is complete: the beggar and the bow are one instrument, and the string's note is the first sound of the new regime.

## The Shot

The shot is the poem's most economical climax. Odysseus draws the arrow from the quiver without rising from his stool, lays it on the bridge, pulls string and arrowhead back together, and releases: the arrow flies through all twelve axe-rings and out the far side, clean. Then he turns to Telemachus with the line that closes the disguise forever: the guest in your hall has not disgraced you — I have not missed the mark, nor did the stringing take me long; my strength stands whole. It is the self-description of the poem's hero, delivered at the one moment when every word of it is proven.

The suitors sit stunned. Outside, the sign they have all missed sounds once more — the thunder. And the book ends on the threshold of the book that will not stop for breath: the doors are barred, the bow is strung, and the man holding it is through with rags.

## Key Themes of Book 21

Book 21 is the poem's exam. The bow is not a weapon but a measurement: it measures the suitors, who fail in a row that reads like a census of their weakness; it measures Telemachus, who nearly strings it and is held back by his father's signal — the poem's quietest proof that the boy has grown; and it measures Odysseus, who strings it without rising from his stool. The contest is Penelope's invention and the poem's verdict in one: a question only the true owner can answer, asked publicly so that the answer is witnessed. And the book's deepest point is the bow's own history — the heirloom of a murdered friend, kept home from Troy — as if the poem wanted the instrument of justice to carry the memory of the world's injustice inside it.
