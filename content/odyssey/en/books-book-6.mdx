## The Princess's Dream

Book 6 opens in the bedchamber of Nausicaa, daughter of Alcinous, asleep in the shape and grace of a goddess. Athena slips into her room in the form of Dymas' daughter, the girl her own age she loves best, and delivers the poem's most domestic divine message: the fine clothes are lying unwashed, and a wedding is not far off — Nausicaa should take the laundry to the river at dawn. Modesty is the joke of the scene. The goddess can say out loud what the girl cannot: marriage is on her mind, and clean clothes are its first requirement.

Nausicaa wakes, asks her father for the mule wagon, and says nothing of her own wedding — she speaks only of her brothers' need for clean clothes. Alcinous understands anyway, and the wagon is loaded with robes, tunics, and olive oil for the bathing.

## Washing at the River

At the washing pools the scene turns idyllic: the maids spread the bright clothes to dry, bathe, oil themselves, and eat a picnic by the river. The picnic ends in a ball game, and the game ends the way all games end in this poem — the ball goes astray. This time the miss is Athena's work: the girls' shriek at the lost throw wakes a man asleep in the thicket nearby, and the poem's two storylines collide on the same beach.

Odysseus has been sleeping in the bushes since his swim ashore, naked and salt-crusted, sheltered by the two entwined shrubs Athena planted around him. The women's cry tells him what he has washed up among. He breaks off a leafy branch to hold before him — the only armor the poem will give him this morning — and steps out.

## The Stranger Emerges

The simile that meets him is famous: he comes out of the bushes like a mountain lion, driven by hunger, rain-slicked and burning-eyed, going among sheep and cattle. The maids scatter down the beach. Only Nausicaa stands her ground — Athena has put courage in her heart — and the meeting that follows is one of the great scenes of tact in the epic: a naked, starving man and a young princess, each working out, sentence by sentence, what the other is owed.

Odysseus does not clasp her knees, the usual supplication — in his state, kneeling might frighten her. He keeps his distance and opens with a question: are you a goddess or a mortal? If mortal, blessed are your parents and brothers; and the man who wins you will be blessed above all. The speech is courtship-shaped on purpose, and it works.

## The Speech to Nausicaa

The heart of the speech is the comparison of Nausicaa to the palm sapling. Odysseus says he has never seen her equal, man or woman, and: "Wait, once I saw the like, in Delos, beside Apollo's altar — the young slip of a palm-tree springing into the light." (Od. 6.162-164) The simile does double work: it praises her beauty while measuring her against a sacred tree at a god's altar — the form of a prayer.

Then the facts: twenty days at sea, driven from Calypso's island, wrecked. He asks for a rag of cloth to cover himself, and the way to town. Nausicaa's answer is everything xenia teaches in three lines: strangers come from Zeus; we give what we can. She calls back her maids — the man is no danger, all wanderers are under Zeus — and orders him bathed, oiled, and clothed.

## The Way into Town

The plan for entering the town is a lesson in local politics. The pair will not walk in together: Odysseus is to follow behind the wagon with the maids until they reach Athena's poplar grove outside the walls, wait there a while, then walk in alone. The Phaeacians are loose-tongued, Nausicaa explains — a girl seen with a foreign man would draw exactly the kind of talk a princess cannot afford. She even scripts the rumors: "who's this tall, handsome stranger with Nausicaa?"

Inside the palace, she tells him, pass by her father and clasp her mother Arete's knees instead: the queen settles disputes and wins respect beyond all women, and her goodwill is the real door into the household. The two part at the grove, and the book ends with the stranger praying to Athena while the princess drives home. The one scene left out — how a beggar crosses the palace threshold — the next book supplies.

## Key Themes of Book 6

Book 6 is the poem's study in aidōs, the modesty that knows what is owed. Every exchange on the beach is measured: the goddess speaks the girl's unspoken wish, the princess covers her own errand with her brothers' laundry, the naked man praises without touching, and the plan for the town protects her reputation to the last detail. The marriage undertone is deliberate — Alcinous will soon offer Nausicaa to the stranger — but the poem keeps it suspended: what happens on this beach is not courtship but its rehearsal. Xenia and eros run side by side here without either being named, and the tact that keeps them apart is the book's real subject.
