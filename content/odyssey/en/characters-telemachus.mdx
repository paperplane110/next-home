## Role in the Epic

Telemachus occupies a position unique in ancient epic: he is the son of a hero who has been absent since the son was an infant. When the Odyssey opens in Book 1, Telemachus is roughly twenty years old. He has never known his father; he has grown up in a house overrun by suitors, where he is the only male member of the royal household present. He is, in the deepest sense, a prince without a kingdom — powerless, passive, quietly consumed by shame and grief. Homer gives us no more acute image of his impotence than the opening scene of Book 1, where Telemachus sits among the suitors in his own palace, "dreaming in his heart how his noble father might come back" and drive the suitors from the halls — dreaming, but doing nothing.

The first four books of the Odyssey, the Telemachy, trace his transformation. By the end of Book 4, he has stood before the Ithacan assembly and denounced the suitors, sailed to two foreign kingdoms in search of news of his father, and returned a changed man. He is no longer a boy. He has earned the right to be called Odysseus' son — not by blood alone, but by his own acts of courage.

## The Telemachy as a Miniature Nostos

Structurally, the Telemachy is the Odyssey in miniature. Where Odysseus journeys over the sea and through the fantastic islands of the western Mediterranean, Telemachus journeys through the Peloponnese — the real, human, fully mapped Greek mainland. Where Odysseus encounters Cyclopes and enchantresses, Telemachus encounters Nestor and Menelaus, human kings who are the guardians of the memory of his father. Where Odysseus' nostos is a return to the past — to the home he left — Telemachus' journey is a journey into the future: into his own manhood, his own authority, his own capacity to rule.

The two journeys converge at the end of Book 16, when father and son meet at last in the hut of Eumaeus the swineherd. The recognition scene is one of the most tender in the Odyssey: Telemachus at first cannot believe that the ragged beggar before him is his father, then flings his arms around Odysseus' neck and weeps. In that embrace, the two nostoi — the father's return home, the son's arrival at manhood — become one.

## The Father-Son Relationship

The relationship between Odysseus and Telemachus develops slowly over the second half of the poem and is never sentimentalized. Homer refuses the easy consolations of reunion. These are two strangers who must, slowly and painfully, learn to be father and son. Telemachus must learn to trust a father he has never known; Odysseus must learn that the son he left as an infant is now a man capable of standing beside him in battle — and of calling him out when his anger threatens to overstep the mark.

Nowhere is this mutual education clearer than in the climactic battle of Book 22. When Odysseus, having killed the suitors' leaders, turns on the disloyal servant girls and orders them hanged, it is Telemachus who quietly intervenes: the servant girls deserve death, but a quick death, not the slow, humiliating hanging his father has ordered. Odysseus, for once, listens. The moment is small but crucial: it marks the point at which the son ceases to be merely the father's instrument and becomes his equal, able to correct and guide him.

## Telemachus and Athena

Like his father, Telemachus enjoys the special protection of Athena. But where Athena and Odysseus are equals in cunning — "we two know how to spin our tricks" — Athena's relationship with Telemachus is explicitly pedagogical. She appears to him in Book 1 disguised as Mentes, explicitly as a teacher: to rouse him from his passivity, to command him to grow up, to show him how a prince speaks and acts and travels. She is, in effect, his surrogate parent during the years of Odysseus' absence. In the Telemachy, Athena is not just a helper; she is the one who creates Telemachus — who makes a hero out of a boy.
