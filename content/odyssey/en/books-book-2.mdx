## The First Assembly in Twenty Years

Book 2 opens with the first assembly of the Ithacan people in twenty years. At dawn Telemachus takes his father's seat as heir of the house — his first public appearance as a man. Old Aegyptius opens the proceedings with the question on everyone's mind: who has called this assembly? No such summons has rung through the square since Odysseus sailed for Troy.

Telemachus' denunciation has two layers: his father's fate unknown, and his estate consumed — "day after day they slaughter my cattle and drink my wine." Midway through, he breaks down, hurls the scepter to the ground, and weeps. The crowd answers with silent pity — the first time the poem lets the people see the state of this household, and the first time Telemachus wins the sympathy of onlookers. But sympathy is not action, and that gap is precisely the political reality this book sets out to show.

## Penelope's Loom: Antinous Strikes Back

Antinous rises and turns the accusation around, laying the blame on Penelope: for three years — soon to be four — she set up a great loom in the hall and wove a shroud for Laertes by day, and unwove it by night; only a serving woman's betrayal exposed the trick. Antinous' conclusion: either Penelope goes back to her father Icarius and chooses a new husband, or the suitors wait forever.

The confession is one of the most famous passages in the poem. Penelope's loom becomes the emblem of her mētis — with day after day of labor she weaves a delay no one can denounce. And Antinous' telling of it betrays him: he has known the truth all along and still does not leave, because what he wants is not the marriage but the wealth of the house.

## The Omen of the Eagles

Telemachus refuses to drive his mother from the house — her father would demand back the bride-price, and she herself would set the Furies on him. No sooner has he spoken than Zeus sends two eagles swooping down from the mountain, tearing at each other's cheeks and necks above the crowd. The old seer Halitherses reads the sign on the spot: Odysseus is near, sowing the seeds of death for the suitors — he will come home in the twentieth year.

Eurymachus answers with a sneer: "Old man, go home and prophesy to your children. There are plenty of birds under the sun, and not every one is fateful." He waves the omen away and mocks the planned voyage in the same breath. This is the book's recurring structure: every manifestation of divine will is deflected by the suitors' arrogance — they do not fail to see the sign; they refuse to accept its consequence.

## Mentor's Rebuke

Telemachus then turns to the people with a request: give him a swift ship and twenty companions, so he can sail to Pylos and Sparta for news of his father; if his father is dead, he will come home, raise a mound, and give his mother to a new husband. At this, old Mentor — the man to whom Odysseus entrusted his house before the war — rises and delivers the sharpest public speech in the poem:

> I am not angry with the suitors — they will pay for it with their own heads. I am angry with all of you: you sit in silence, and not one of you rises to stop them.

The speech is the political heart of Book 2, and of the whole Telemachy: wrongdoing persists not only through the wrongdoers but through the silence of bystanders. Leocritus then threatens that even Odysseus himself would die at the suitors' hands if he came home, and dissolves the assembly. The first assembly in twenty years ends in nothing.

## The Secret Departure

After the assembly, Telemachus walks alone to the seashore, washes his hands in the grey salt water, and prays to Athena. The goddess appears beside him in Mentor's shape with two promises: she will fit out a ship herself and sail with him, and his father's daring runs in his blood. Telemachus "knew the goddess in his heart," and hesitates no longer.

What follows is a compact secret operation: back at the palace he endures the suitors' mockery ("may Zeus destroy his strength before he reaches manhood" — another taunt about growing up), then quietly tells the old nurse Eurycleia to prepare wine and barley, binding her by oath not to breathe a word before the twelfth day unless his mother asks. Meanwhile Athena, in the likeness of Telemachus, walks through the town recruiting twenty willing oarsmen and borrows a swift ship from Noemon. At sundown the ship is launched and rigged; libations are poured, Athena sends a favoring wind, and the boat leaves Ithaca in the dark, bound for Pylos.

## Key Themes of Book 2

Book 2 is the political lesson of the Telemachy. It carries the private awakening of Book 1 into the public square: a boy speaks for the first time, is mocked for the first time, wins pity for the first time, and meets the silence of bystanders for the first time. The three speeches of Antinous, Eurymachus, and Mentor map the poem's moral spectrum: the defense of evil, the indifference to evil, and the rebuke of silence. Penelope's loom reminds us that long before Telemachus opened his mouth, someone in this house had already found another way to resist. And the secret departure binds the two generations' mētis into one line: Athena acts in his likeness, while he acts with his father's caution.
