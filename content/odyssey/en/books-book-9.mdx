## The Name Revealed

The answer to Alcinous' question comes at the top of Book 9, and it is the first time since the poem began that the hero can say who he is. "I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, known to the world for every kind of craft — my fame has reached the skies." (Od. 9.19-20) He names his home, Ithaca with its mountain Neriton, and then says the thing the poem has spent eight books building toward: Calypso held him, Circe held him, but nothing held him long, because nothing can beat a man's homeland. From here the voice belongs to the hero; the next four books are his story in the first person.

## The Raid on Ismarus

The story begins where the war ended: Troy. The wind took the fleet to Ismarus, city of the Cicones, Troy's allies. The raid succeeds — city sacked, women and treasure taken — and then comes the argument that will define the crew: Odysseus says sail now, the men say feast. They stay, and at dawn the Cicones return with inland allies, as thick as leaves. The battle lasts a full day, and by evening each ship has lost six men. The first adventure is already the lesson the crew will never learn: plunder invites counter-plunder, and the appetite that refuses to leave is paid for in dead men.

## The Lotus-Eaters

A nine-day storm drives the ships off course — the first of the winds Zeus spends on this crew — and on the tenth day they land among the Lotus-Eaters. The people are gentle; their danger is their food. Whoever eats the honeyed lotus fruit forgets homecoming and wants only to stay and graze on lotus forever. The scouts who taste it must be dragged back weeping, bound under the rowing benches. What the Lotus-Eaters threaten is subtler than a monster: the loss of the desire to return, which for this poem is the same thing as the loss of self.

## In the Cave of Polyphemus

Then the Cyclopes' land: the island of wild goats offshore, and across the strait the great cave of Polyphemus, son of Poseidon — a lawless giant who does not fear Zeus, trusting the gods to keep him. Odysseus takes twelve men and a goatskin of Maron's wine — Maron, priest of Apollo at Ismarus — so strong that one cup took twenty of water. The cave is stocked with cheese and lambs; the men want to raid and run; Odysseus wants to see the cave-dweller. This time his curiosity pays in dead men.

Polyphemus returns with the flocks, rolls a boulder over the mouth that no crew could budge, and finds the strangers. His greeting is the parody of xenia — who are you? — and his guest-gift is to eat two men raw, like a mountain lion, bones and all. The door is a stone no one can move. Odysseus' mind, which the poem calls craft, begins to work.

## Nobody: The Wine, the Stake, and the Rams

The plan has three steps, each a piece of the trickster's trade. The wine: Polyphemus drinks three bowls and asks the stranger's name, so he can give a guest-gift in return. "Nobody — that's my name. Nobody — so my mother and father call me, all my friends." (Od. 9.366-367) The stake: they sharpen a great beam of olive wood and hide it in the dung. The timing: the giant vomits and sleeps. The men drive the heated stake into the single eye, and the scream brings the other Cyclopes to the door — but the answer, "Nobody's killing me," sends them away. The trick wins twice: the name hides the man, and the false name hides the truth in plain hearing.

The escape is the third step: Odysseus ties the rams in threes and slings a man under the middle ram of each trio. Polyphemus feels the backs of the rams as they go out, and the poem gives him, monstrous as he is, the book's tenderest speech — the giant's address to his favorite ram, grieving for his master's eye, not knowing the ram carries the enemy beneath. The last touch is the taunt from the ship, once the escape is safe and pride undoes craft.

## The Curse

The taunt is the book's one true mistake, and Odysseus narrates it without excusing himself: he could not resist shouting. Polyphemus hurls the mountaintop, and then prays to his father Poseidon: let the man never reach home — or if it is his fate to reach it, let it be late, after he has lost all his companions, in another's ship, and let him find trouble in his house. Poseidon hears, and the rest of the Odyssey is the working-out of that prayer. The Cyclops' curse is the poem's plot, delivered by the victim.

## Key Themes of Book 9

Book 9 is the poem's handbook of craft and its limits. The name-trick, the stake, the rams: intelligence wins every round against strength, and the book enjoys the winning. But it also draws the line: mētis cannot govern the mouth. The one moment the hero stops calculating — the shout from the ship — costs him ten years. And beneath the adventure runs the counter-story of the crew: at Ismarus they feast when they should sail, here Odysseus lingers when he should run. Appetite and curiosity are the same flaw in different men, and Book 9 opens the accounting for both.
