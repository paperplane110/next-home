## The First Song

At dawn Alcinous calls the assembly and announces the escort: fifty-two picked oarsmen will carry the stranger home. A ship is rigged in the harbor, and the court assembles in the hall, where the blind bard Demodocus — the man the Muse loved and blinded, giving him sweet song in place of sight — takes up the lyre. His first song is the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles at Troy. At the first notes, Odysseus pulls his purple cloak over his head and weeps.

Only Alcinous notices. He sits beside the stranger, and a host who has not yet asked his guest's name does the next best thing: he stops the song and calls for games, so that the man may learn how the Phaeacians excel.

## The Games and the Taunt

The games follow: footrace, wrestling, jumping, discus, boxing. Laodamas, the king's favorite son, invites the stranger to try — games, he says, are what make a man's name. Odysseus declines: his heart is worn out, and he wants only to go home. Then Euryalus turns the invitation into a taunt: the stranger is no athlete, he says, but some skipper of a merchant crew, counting his cargo, a man whose eyes never leave his profit.

The insult is the book's hinge. Odysseus answers it by standing up, still wrapped in his cloak, and drawing the line between what a man is and what he looks like: the gods give one man beauty and another words, and the plain-looking man can speak so that people gaze at him with delight. He seizes the heaviest discus and throws it past every mark; Athena, in a Phaeacian's shape, marks the spot and tells him even a blind man could find it.

## The Discus and the Rebuke

The throw turns the games into a challenge. Let any of them come forward, Odysseus says — boxing, wrestling, running, all of them except Laodamas, his host, for a man does not compete with the one who welcomed him. In archery, he adds, only Philoctetes outshot him at Troy. The hall quiets. Alcinous answers with the Phaeacians' own excellences: they are no great boxers or wrestlers, he says, but runners and sailors, and lovers of feasts, lyre, dancing, fresh clothes, warm baths, and beds.

Then the dancers come out, Laodamas and Halius with the purple ball, and the lords load the stranger with gifts: cloak and tunic, a talent of gold each, and from Euryalus an apology in the form of a bronze sword with silver-studded hilt and ivory sheath. The taunter is the first to give — the poem's way of closing a quarrel with gifts rather than blows. After the bath, Nausicaa meets him at the gate: when he is home, she tells him, he should remember her — he owes his life to her. He answers that he will pray to her as a goddess, all his days.

## The Song of Ares and Aphrodite

Back at the feast, Demodocus sings again, this time a story the poem clearly enjoys: Ares and Aphrodite in Hephaestus' bed, betrayed by the sun god Helios, who reports the adultery to the lame smith. Hephaestus rigs the famous net, invisible chains finer than spider-silk, and springs it; the two lovers lie trapped while Hephaestus calls all the gods to look, and the deathless gods shake with laughter at the door. Poseidon stands surety and pays the price of Ares' release, and the lovers go their separate ways.

The song's function in the hall is diplomacy: it makes the company laugh, which after the discus is what the feast needs. Its deeper note is the joke the gods enjoy — that desire, caught in the open, is funny to everyone but the man in the bed.

## The Wooden Horse and the Widow

Odysseus, who has heard his own deeds sung, praises Demodocus and calls for one more song: the wooden horse, built with Athena's help, by which Troy fell. Demodocus sings it — the Achaeans pouring from the horse, the city burning. Odysseus weeps the way he wept for the first song, and the poem gives his grief the famous simile: "As a woman weeps, her arms flung round her darling husband, a man who fell in battle, fighting for town and townsmen" — so Odysseus wept (Od. 8.523-526). His tears are the only identity he has offered all evening.

Again only Alcinous sees. He stops the singer — enough of this song, it brings the guest no joy — and asks, at last, the question the whole Phaeacian episode has been postponing: who are you? What land, what people, what name did your parents give you? No man is nameless, he says; there is no man alive without a name. The question hands the poem to its speaker: the next four books are the answer.

## Key Themes of Book 8

Book 8 is the poem's reflection on its own medium. A bard sings the war to a man who fought it, and the man weeps: kleos, the glory that song preserves, turns out to cost its owner the grief it spares the audience. The games set the same question in another key — what a man is worth cannot be read from his looks or his clothes, and Euryalus learns the lesson with a sword in his hand. And the structure of the whole book points one way: three songs, three floods of tears, then the name. The Phaeacian episode has asked, at every turn, who this stranger is; the answer begins in the next book, in the first person.
