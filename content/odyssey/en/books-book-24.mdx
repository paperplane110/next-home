## The Ghosts of the Suitors

The last book opens underground. Hermes leads the suitors' souls down to the underworld, and the poem gives the procession its strangest simile: they follow the god the way bats follow each other through a cave, squeaking, clinging in chains. The dead go past the stream of Oceanus, the rock, the gates of the sun, the land of dreams, and come to the asphodel meadow, where Achilles and Agamemnon are still talking of old wars.

The two heroes measure their deaths against each other — Agamemnon's inglorious murder against Achilles' funeral, the greatest the world has seen — and then the suitors arrive, and the dead of Troy confront the dead of Ithaca. The poem has saved one last audience scene for the underworld: the men who died in the hero's war meet the men who died in his hall, and the difference between the two deaths is the whole of the poem's morality.

## Amphimedon's Report

Agamemnon asks the suitors' spokesman what brought them down, so many young men at once, and Amphimedon tells the story the poem has just finished telling: the weaving trick, the bow, the beggar, the slaughter. It is the Odyssey retold in thirty lines by one of its villains, and it lands on Agamemnon with a force the dead king cannot contain. He thinks of his own wife, the bath, the ax — and measures her against the woman Amphimedon has described, and breaks out in the poem's final judgment on its two queens: happy Odysseus, he says, you won a wife of great character. The fame of her great virtue will never die (Od. 24.192-198); the gods themselves will make a song for Penelope, while Clytemnestra's name will be a curse on all women.

The verdict comes from the one man in the poem entitled to give it — the husband killed by his wife, speaking from the dead. The Odyssey's last word on Penelope is spoken by the poem's worst-case husband, and it is the word the whole poem has been building toward: fidelity will be sung.

## The Old Man in the Orchard

The poem then rises to the surface, to the farm of Laertes. The old king lives like a laborer — patched clothes, leather gaiters against the thorns — and Odysseus finds him alone in the orchard, digging around a vine. He has come, he says, to test his father, and the test is the poem's last: he pretends to be a stranger who hosted Odysseus five years ago, and asks after him as if asking after the dead. The effect on the old man is the book's cruelest kindness: Laertes weeps, throws dust over his white head, and the poem says the sight of his father's grief broke Odysseus past bearing.

He drops the ruse, and offers the two proofs that belong to this recognition alone: the scar, and the orchard — the thirteen pear trees, the ten apple trees, the forty figs, the fifty rows of vines his father gave him as a child, when the boy ran after him through the rows asking the name of everything. The old man's knees give way; he throws his arms around his son. And Laertes says the line that closes the poem's long argument with the gods: if the suitors are really dead, he says, then the gods still live on Olympus. The poem's last nostos — the father's recovery of his son — is complete.

## The Kinsmen Rise

But the poem is not finished with blood. Rumor runs through the town, and Eupeithes, Antinous' father, rouses the kinsmen: the man who killed our sons, he says, has come home, and if we do not strike now, he will sail away with his crews and the shame will be on us forever. Half the town arms. The old seer Halitherses argues against the rising — the suitors earned their deaths, and the gods were in it; Medon, returned from the palace, bears witness to what he saw — but the grief of fathers is not reasoned with. The kinsmen march on the farm.

The battle is joined, and it is the poem's last irony that the first man to fall is Eupeithes, killed by a spear-cast from Laertes — the old king's first kill since his youth, and the poem lets the father of the murdered suitor die at the hands of the father of the avenger. The symmetry is exact: the poem's accounting runs in generations.

## Athena's Peace

The fight is about to become a war — the kinsmen charge, Odysseus and his men meet them — when the poem's machinery of peace arrives. Zeus hurls the thunderbolt at Athena's feet, and the goddess cries out in Mentor's voice: hold! End the war, she says; let no more blood be spilled. The kinsmen pale and flee; Odysseus leaps after them like an eagle, and Zeus's thunderbolt falls again and stops him in his tracks. Then Athena, in her own form, speaks the covenant: Odysseus, son of Laertes, hold back; end the quarrel. Let both sides swear oaths of peace — let the king reign, and let the dead be forgotten.

And the poem ends, famously, at full stride: the two sides swore, the goddess made them swear. The Odyssey closes not with the hero's triumph but with an armistice — the blood-feud halted by divine decree, the cycle that would have gone on forever cut by a thunderbolt. It is the poem's last and strangest judgment: justice done is one thing; justice continuing is another, and the gods will have it stop.

## Key Themes of Book 24

Book 24 is the poem's epilogue, and it works by doubling. The underworld scene replays the whole story in the mouths of the dead — Amphimedon's report, Agamemnon's verdict — so that the tale we have followed is judged by its own characters before it is finished. The Laertes scene replays the recognitions of the second half, with the proofs that belong to a father: the scar, and the trees he planted in his son. And the battle replays the slaughter, and is stopped where the slaughter was not — the poem's final word is not victory but peace, imposed from above. The Odyssey has always known that killing the suitors answers one question and opens another: who stops the fathers? Its answer is the gods, with a thunderbolt and an oath. The poem that began with a man trying to get home ends with a covenant that lets him stay there.
