## The Sleepless King

Odysseus lies in the portico, and sleep will not come. The maids slip out one by one to the suitors' beds, laughing, and his heart growls inside him like a bitch standing over her pups, baring her teeth at a stranger. He beats his chest and gives the poem's most famous order to himself: bear up, old heart — you have borne worse. The line is the whole return in miniature: the dog that would kill is told to wait.

Athena comes and closes the question with a goddess's logic: you are home now — sleep. And on the other side of the house, Penelope wakes and weeps and prays to Artemis: let me die now, before I must lie beside a lesser man; or send me a dream. The dream comes at once — the image of her husband, like him in all things — and the poem lets the two of them sleep, the king and the queen, separated by a wall, each watched by the same goddess.

## The Thunder and the Mill-Woman

At dawn Penelope's weeping wakes Odysseus, and he prays aloud to Zeus: give me a sign. The poem answers from a clear sky — Zeus thunders once, out of nowhere, over the house — and the book marks it as the omen the king asked for. Then comes the second sign, from the humblest source in the palace: a mill-woman, the weakest of the serving women, still grinding at her quern long past the others' strength, hears the thunder and prays: Zeus, let this be the last day the suitors feast in this house. The poem's arithmetic of witness is complete: the king above, the slave below, the thunder between them. The house of Odysseus has heard its own sentence, and no suitor in it has woken.

## Philoetius the Cowherd

The feast day of Apollo brings the household's last loyal piece into place. Philoetius the cowherd arrives with a cow, greets the beggar with the kindness the poem reserves for its faithful — you have the look of a king, old man, he says — and stands talking with him at the gate. Odysseus tests him as he tested Eumaeus: if Odysseus came back, would you fight? Philoetius' answer is the oath the poem has been collecting all along: may Zeus bring it about — and you would see the strength in these hands.

Then the beggar speaks the night's first open truth: since you are the man you seem, hear this — Odysseus will come home this very day, and you will be here to see the suitors fall. The cowherd and the swineherd both pray for it. The company of the loyal is assembled at last — Eumaeus, Philoetius, Eurycleia — and the poem has given each of them the same secret in a different key.

## The Laughter of the Damned

The feast begins, and the book turns to its strangest page. Ctesippus throws an ox-hoof at the beggar, and Telemachus' rebuke is a man's rebuke now — count yourself lucky, he says, that the hoof went wide. Agelaus rises with the suitors' final argument, the one that sounds most like reason: tell your mother to marry the best of us; we cannot keep eating our own inheritances while she delays. It is the suitors' economics in its purest form — they have eaten the king's estate and present the bill to the queen.

And then Athena sends the laughter. It seizes the suitors all at once, a laughter that is not their own: their faces twist, they laugh with tears running down their cheeks, and the meat before them, the poem says, is dabbled with blood. They laugh the way the poem's doomed always laugh — at the wrong thing, in the wrong place, an hour before the door closes.

## Theoclymenus' Vision

One man in the hall sees what the laughter means. Theoclymenus the seer stands up among them: the walls and the handsome crossbeams dripping dank with blood (Od. 20.351-357), he says — ghosts thronging the porch and court, the sun blotted from the sky. The suitors laugh at him too; Eurymachus calls him mad and offers to throw him out. The seer answers with the poem's most composed exit: I will go myself, he says — I see your doom. He leaves for Piraeus' house, the last man out before the blood, and the book ends with the suitors still laughing, still feasting, under a roof that has already been measured for their deaths.

## Key Themes of Book 20

Book 20 is the poem's book of omens — the last quiet day, filled with signs that all point one way. The thunder, the mill-woman's prayer, the cowherd's oath, the blood on the meat, the vision in the hall: the poem lines up its witnesses and counts them, the way a house counts its own before a fire. Against the signs it sets the suitors' blindness, which by now has passed from vice into something the gods can use: Athena sends the laughter itself. The last feast of the suitors is a festival of misreading — they read the omen as madness, the seer as a fool, the beggar as a nuisance — and the poem's judgment on them is already in the air: the day before the bow is the last day anyone in this hall misreads anything.
