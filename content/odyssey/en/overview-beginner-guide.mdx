## Before You Start: Three Things to Know

The Odyssey can be summarized in one sentence: after the fall of Troy, the Greek hero Odysseus offends Poseidon on his way home and is driven across the sea for ten years, while back on Ithaca his palace is occupied by more than a hundred suitors and his wife Penelope and son Telemachus hold on, waiting for him. The whole poem asks a single question: can he get home — and what happens when he does.

You do not need to do homework before reading. The cast that actually matters is small: on the mortal side, the family of three plus a few rivals; on the divine side, Athena (who helps Odysseus throughout), Poseidon (who obstructs him throughout), and Zeus (who settles the matter in the end). Everyone else can be looked up as you go — that is what this wiki is for.

One reassurance up front: this is not a difficult book. Its rhythm is closer to a serial adventure drama, and every book has a clear dramatic job. What trips up first-time readers is rarely the plot; it is the unfamiliar names and places. They are not a barrier to understanding — they just take a little time to become familiar.

## The Map: Where the Story Happens

The stage of the Odyssey is a smaller sea than you might imagine: the Aegean. Three coordinates locate the whole story:

- to the west, Odysseus' home: the island of Ithaca off the west coast of Greece, facing the Ionian Sea;
- in the middle, the Greek mainland and the Peloponnese — Mycenae, Sparta, and Pylos are all here;
- to the east, across the Aegean, the northwest corner of Asia Minor (the Asian part of modern Turkey) — where Troy sits, commanding the straits that lead to the Black Sea.

For a sense of scale: the Aegean is roughly two to three times the size of the Bohai Sea, and the straight-line distance from mainland Greece to Troy is only about three hundred kilometers — roughly the distance from Shanghai to Nanjing. The "expedition" and the "wandering" of the epic are not an ocean crossing; they are repeated zigzags across an island-dotted inland sea.

## The Timeline: China as a Reference Point

For readers who know Chinese history, this is the fastest way to locate the story in time:

- the Trojan War (the story's setting): late 13th century BCE — traditional dating places the fall of Troy around 1184 BCE, which falls in the late Shang dynasty, a century and a bit before King Wu's conquest of the Shang (c. 1046 BCE);
- the Mycenaean world (the Greece of the story): c. 1600–1100 BCE, almost exactly parallel to the entire Shang dynasty. After its collapse, Greece entered a "Dark Age" without writing — a period that corresponds to the Western Zhou in China;
- the composition of the epics (when they were written down): c. 8th century BCE, which corresponds to the end of the Western Zhou and the start of the Spring and Autumn period.

One vivid parallel: while Homer was shaping the four-hundred-year-old story of Troy into epic verse on the Aegean coast, people along the Yellow River were singing the songs that would later be collected in the Book of Songs — East and West's "earliest poetry" are roughly contemporary. And note that Homer is not telling a story of his own time: he is writing about an age four centuries before him, which is like a modern author writing a novel set in the Ming dynasty.

## Greek Myth and This Book

A common beginner's question: does Greek mythology "come from" the Homeric epics? The answer: Homer is one of the most important literary sources of Greek myth — but not the only one, and certainly not a "scripture." Greek myth never had a scripture.

- before Homer, the Linear B tablets of the Mycenaean palaces already name Zeus, Poseidon, and Athena — but the tablets contain only accounts and inventories, no stories;
- beside Homer stands the other great poet, Hesiod, whose Theogony tells the genealogy of the gods;
- widen the view to the whole Mediterranean, and the Epic of Gilgamesh from Mesopotamia predates Homer by some thirteen hundred years — the oldest surviving heroic epic in the world. Homer stands on the shoulders of a much older eastern tradition.

In short: calling Homer "the beginning of Western literature" is fair, but Greek myth itself is older than he is.

## How Far Did Odysseus Drift? A Route in Brief

Odysseus' ten-year wandering runs from Troy to Ithaca. The main stops, in order:

- Ismarus: a raid on the Cicones and the first loss of men;
- the Land of the Lotus-Eaters: eat the lotus and forget home — the temptation of oblivion;
- the land of the Cyclops: Odysseus blinds Polyphemus and escapes, offending the giant's father Poseidon and earning the decade of suffering;
- the island of Aeolus: the wind-god bags the adverse winds; the crew opens the bag near home and is blown back to the start;
- the land of the Laestrygonians: cannibal giants sink nearly the whole fleet;
- Aeaea: the witch Circe turns the men into pigs; with Hermes' herb Odysseus prevails and stays a year;
- the Underworld: to consult the prophet Tiresias about the way home;
- the Sirens: their song lures sailors onto the rocks; Odysseus listens lashed to the mast;
- Scylla and Charybdis: a six-headed monster on one side, a swallowing whirlpool on the other;
- Thrinacia: the starving crew slaughters the cattle of the sun-god Helios; Zeus strikes the ship and all are lost but Odysseus;
- Ogygia: held for seven years by the nymph Calypso;
- Scheria: the Phaeacians hear the whole story and carry him home.

Two cautions: first, most of these places sit between the real and the invented (scholars have mapped the later stages toward southern Italy and Sicily), so do not try to verify each one on a modern map. Second, note the pacing: these twelve stops are compressed into Books 9–12 of a 24-book poem, and the remaining twelve books all take place at home. The real drama is not at sea — it is in the halls of Ithaca.

## How the War Ended: Winners Who Came Home Badly

The Trojan War ended in a Greek victory: after ten years of siege, the city fell to the stratagem of the wooden horse, Odysseus' plan. Troy was sacked and burned; the old king Priam died at the altar.

But what the epic really wants to tell begins after the victory. The Greeks' arrogance during the sack offended the gods, and the homecomings of the winners went badly, one after another: Agamemnon, the commander, was murdered the moment he arrived home, by his wife and her lover; Ajax the Lesser drowned at sea; Odysseus wandered for ten years. The first four books of the Odyssey keep measuring three homecomings against one another: Agamemnon is the homecoming that failed, Menelaus of Sparta the homecoming that succeeded, and Odysseus the homecoming still in progress. The poem's question is therefore not "can we win," but "after the war is over, can a man become himself again."

## Three Tips for a First Reading

- Read with the structure. The 24 books fall into four movements: Books 1–4 follow the son, Telemachus, as he sails in search of news (a first look at the household); Books 5–8 bring Odysseus onstage, leaving Calypso and arriving among the Phaeacians; Books 9–12 are the wanderings told in his own voice (the most adventure-novel part of the poem); Books 13–24 return to Ithaca — disguise, the bow, the slaughter of the suitors, and the reunion. If you want the adventures first, start at Book 9 and double back for the setup.
- Hold on to three keywords, and nearly every episode becomes their footnote: xenia (the law of hospitality — the line that separates good from evil in this poem), nostos (homecoming — the engine of the whole epic), and mētis (cunning intelligence — the quality Odysseus and Athena share).
- Do not be afraid of the repetitions. Set phrases like "rosy-fingered dawn" and "wine-dark sea" return again and again; that is the oral tradition at work, not padding. Take it as the rhythm of waves — the actual density of the storytelling is high.
