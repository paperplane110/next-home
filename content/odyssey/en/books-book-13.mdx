## The Farewell Feast

Book 13 opens with the leave-taking that closes the Phaeacian episode: the lords load the ship with bronze, gold, and woven cloth, and Alcinous hosts one last feast, a bull for Zeus. Odysseus makes his parting toast to Arete — while you live, I will pray to you as a goddess — and sets out. The crossing is the gift the Phaeacians are famous for: their ships need no pilots and feel their own way across the sea. Odysseus sleeps on the deck, and the poem gives him the most loaded description of rest in the epic: a sleep like death, sweet and sound, the answer to every night he has spent awake since Troy.

At dawn the ship makes the harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, at the island's head. The sailors carry the sleeping man ashore with all his gifts, pile them by the sacred olive, and sail for home. Odysseus is on Ithaca before he has opened his eyes on it.

## The Ship of Stone

The crossing that ends the hero's wanderings ends something else too. Poseidon, watching the ship return, complains to Zeus: the Phaeacians escort every stranger, and the honor due the god of the sea has been stripped away. Zeus grants him his spectacle: Poseidon strikes the returning ship and turns it to stone in the harbor's mouth, rooted to the seabed. The Phaeacians watch from shore, remember the old prophecy — their father Nausithous had warned that Poseidon would one day petrify a ship and ring the city with mountains — and understand: the age of escorts is over. They sacrifice to Poseidon and vow to convey strangers no more.

The poem closes its most utopian society with a single act of divine spite. The price of Odysseus' passage was always going to be paid by someone; the Phaeacians pay it in stone.

## A Stranger in His Own Land

Odysseus wakes to a land he does not recognize. Athena has poured mist over Ithaca so that he can learn its state before it knows him — and the poem plays its favorite irony for one long scene: the hero home at last, counting his gifts on the beach, weeping because he believes himself dumped on a foreign shore by faithless sailors. He does not know the harbor, the olive, the caves. Twenty years have made him a stranger to his own kingdom.

Athena approaches in the shape of a young shepherd prince, and Odysseus, wary as ever, answers her questions with his first full Cretan lie: he is a fugitive from Crete, he says, a man who killed a raider in an ambush and fled with a Phoenician ship, left on this shore by sailors against his will. The goddess listens to the whole performance, and then she smiles.

## The First Cretan Lie

Athena's reaction to the lie is one of the poem's most affectionate scenes. She drops her disguise — the shepherd boy becomes a tall, beautiful woman — and tells him, with something like admiration, that any man or god who met him would have to be a champion liar to get past him, since his craft and his guile never fail. You have not recognized your own land, she says, and you did not stop weaving even on its shore. It is the only moment in the epic where a god congratulates a mortal on lying.

She names the land — Ithaca — and Odysseus kisses the earth. Then the goddess and the hero sit down under the olive tree like two old campaigners and take stock: the suitors, three years in the house, devouring the estate; the gifts to be hidden in the cave of the nymphs; Telemachus in Sparta, to be called home. The poem's second half is planned in a single conversation.

## The Beggar's Disguise

The plan begins with the poem's signature device: disguise. Athena shrivels the skin on Odysseus' limbs, strips his hair, dims his eyes, and wraps him in rags — he becomes the old beggar the palace will see for the next nine books. The transformation is the moral of the whole return: before the king reclaims his house, he will walk through it as the least man in it, and every hand extended or withheld will be entered in the book of the dead.

The goddess sends him up the mountain path to the hut of the swineherd Eumaeus — the first Ithacan he will meet, and the one the poem calls "his most loyal man." Book 14 is the test of that loyalty, and of the disguise that will test everyone else.

## Key Themes of Book 13

Book 13 is the hinge of the Odyssey. The poem's first half — wanderings, monsters, gods — ends on this shore; its second half — a house, a wife, a bow — begins with a disguised king. The hinge turns on two of the poem's deepest convictions. One: the truth is best approached in disguise, and the liar's craft, in this poem, is a form of wisdom — the goddess of wisdom herself admires it. Two: homecoming is not the end of the journey but the beginning of its hardest part. Odysseus has crossed the sea; now he must cross the threshold of his own house, and the poem will spend the rest of itself on that short distance.
