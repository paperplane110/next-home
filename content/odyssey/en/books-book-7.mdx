## Through Athena's Mist

Odysseus prays in the poplar grove while Nausicaa drives home, and Athena answers him in the form of a young girl carrying a pitcher. The disguise is the poem's kindness to a man who has just been stripped of everything: she meets him, hears him out, and walks him through the city wrapped in mist — no Phaeacian will see him, question him, or mock him before he has clothes and a meal. Even so, she warns him to speak to no one on the way: the Phaeacians, she says, do not much care for strangers.

Her counsel repeats Nausicaa's: pass by Alcinous and clasp the queen's knees first. Arete, descended from the line of Rhexenor, is honored beyond all women; she settles the disputes even of men, and if her heart is warm toward him, there is hope of home. Then the mist thins, and the palace appears.

## A Palace of Bronze and Gold

The description of Alcinous' palace is the poem's one sustained piece of utopian architecture. Bronze walls run up from the threshold with a cornice of blue enamel; gold and silver doors; and guarding the doors, gold and silver dogs, the work of Hephaestus, immortal and ageless. Inside, seats along the walls and boys on pedestals holding torches for the feasts at night.

Behind the palace lies the famous orchard, where pears and pomegranates, apples and figs ripen without season — the west wind is always blowing there, budding some fruits and ripening others, so that the garden bears all year round. Beside it, a vineyard and two springs. This is the poem's picture of a society the gods have chosen to spare: not wealth alone, but wealth that renews itself without effort, as if nature had agreed to keep house for the king.

## Before the Queen: Arete

Odysseus enters the hall still wrapped in mist, crosses it while the Phaeacian lords sit over their wine, and drops at Arete's knees. The mist dissolves at the touch of his supplication, and the hall goes silent. The moment belongs to the old lord Echeneus, who finds the words the court is too startled to find: it is not right, he says, for a stranger to sit in the ashes of the hearth. Seat him, serve him. Alcinous lifts Odysseus up and seats him beside his favorite son, Laodamas; the feast is served.

Only after the libations to Zeus does the question of identity come near, and it comes sideways: Arete notices the clothes on the stranger's back — they are from her own household, Nausicaa's weaving. Who gave them to him? Odysseus answers with the story of Calypso and the sea, withholding his name. The poem has kept the name back for three books now; the queen's sharp eye nearly pulls it out of him, but he turns the question and keeps his secret.

## The Promise of Passage

Alcinous hears the account and does what no one else in the poem's first half can do: he promises passage home, without ransom, without delay. He goes further — a man like this, he says, is the son-in-law he would wish for, and he would give him house and wealth if he stayed. The offer hangs in the air, and the poem lets it: marriage to Nausicaa, wealth in Phaeacia, rest at the price of staying.

The night ends with Odysseus in the portico on a bed of hides, and the household asleep. The next morning will bring the assembly, the ship, and the question the book has carefully postponed: who is this man?

## Key Themes of Book 7

Book 7 is a study of the stranger's entrance. Everything in it is threshold: the mist, the gate, the knees, the hearth, the question deferred. Arete's authority is the book's quiet novelty — the poem insists, twice over, that the way to this king passes through his queen, and that a household's welcome is first of all the woman's work. The palace itself is set up as the opposite of every place Odysseus has lately been: no monsters, no storms, no hunger, a garden that bears in all seasons. And yet the offer that crowns the book — stay, marry, rest — follows Calypso's immortality as the second great temptation of the poem's first half. The stranger's refusal is not spoken yet, but the question is already on the table.
