## The Road to Town: Melanthius

Telemachus goes ahead to the palace, where Penelope meets him with tears and questions and he tells her the careful half-truth: Menelaus, he says, had heard that Odysseus was alive, held by Calypso on an island. Theoclymenus says more than the son dares: Odysseus is already in Ithaca, the seer declares, sowing destruction. The queen answers: may it prove true.

Behind them, the beggar and Eumaeus walk the mountain road, and the poem sets their welcome in its ugliest form: Melanthius the goatherd drives his goats past and curses them both — the swineherd leading a starving beggar to gnaw at the suitors' tables — and kicks Odysseus on the hip as he passes. The king stands his ground and endures it, weighing in his mind whether to strike the man dead then and there. The poem marks the decision: he endures. The hall will ask this of him all day, and he has decided what the answer will be.

## Argos

At the palace gate the poem pays out one of its most famous scenes, built on nothing but a dog. Argos, Odysseus' hound, lies on a dung heap at the gate, old, flea-ridden, abandoned since his master sailed: he was the fastest of the pack in the old days, and no one hunts with him now. As the two men approach, Argos pricks his ears. The poem does not need the dog to recognize the master by sight — he knows the voice, the step, twenty years later: he dropped his ears and wagged his tail (Od. 17.326), and had no strength left to drag himself nearer. Odysseus wipes away a tear, turns to Eumaeus with a question that hides the thing he cannot say — it is strange, he says, that such a dog should lie in the dirt; he must have been a fine one once — and passes through the gate. The dark shadow of death closed on Argos the moment he had seen his master, the poem says, in the twentieth year. It is the poem's most economical recognition: the one creature in Ithaca that needs no proof, and dies the instant the proof is given.

## Antinous' Stool

Inside the hall, the beggar works his way down the suitors' tables, and the poem lets the custom of xenia do its sorting. Most of the suitors give — scraps, from full plates, which is what the law requires of them. Antinous, the law's best enemy, refuses and adds abuse: what god sent this nuisance to gnaw at our feast? He calls Eumaeus back to account for bringing him. The beggar's answer is the book's plainest speech: I would work if you gave me work, he says — I can serve, build, keep a farm; and in any contest of arms I would hold my own. But you, Antinous, who take what belongs to others and call it your own feast, are the one who cannot spare a crust — a king's goods in your hands, and still you are the poorest man at the table.

Antinous answers with the stool, hurled at the beggar's shoulder. Odysseus takes it without flinching, like a rock in the sea, shakes his head in silence, and the poem says it: he was brooding on the suitors' doom.

## The Beggar's Curse

Then the beggar speaks his curse into the hall: if there are gods and Furies for beggars, may death come to Antinous before his wedding. It is the first death named in the house of Odysseus, spoken by the man who will carry it out. The other suitors are appalled at Antinous — the beggar might be a god in disguise, they say; the gods do walk the earth in strangers' shape, testing men's deeds. The poem's audience knows how right they are, and how little it will help them.

Penelope, from her room, hears that a stranger has been struck in her hall. It is shame, she says, that a guest should be abused in this house; call him to me. The interview she wants is the poem's great scene of the second half, and it must wait for night: the beggar will not meet the queen while the suitors watch. Evening falls on the first day of the king in his own hall.

## Key Themes of Book 17

Book 17 is the poem's study of endurance. The disguised king enters his house as its least man, and the book walks him through every insult the house can offer — the goatherd's kick, the stool, the scraps — and records what he does with each: he endures, and he stores it. Endurance here is not passivity; it is the discipline of the avenger, who must let every wrong be committed before he collects. Against his silence the poem sets its two witnesses: Argos, who recognizes him and dies, and Penelope, who does not recognize him and yet defends the stranger as if she did. The dog and the queen hold the two ends of the recognition to come: one needs no proof, the other will need the greatest proof in the poem.
