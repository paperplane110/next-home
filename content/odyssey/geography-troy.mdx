## The Site of Troy

Troy — known to the Greeks as Ilion (Ἴλιον) and Troia (Τροία), and to the Hittites as Wilusa — stood on the mound of Hisarlık in the northwestern corner of Anatolia, overlooking the narrow straits that the Greeks called the Hellespont (the Dardanelles). Its position was strategic: it controlled the maritime passage between the Aegean Sea and the Black Sea, and the overland route between Europe and Asia. For a Bronze Age kingdom, this was a position of immense commercial and military value. Troy grew wealthy on trade and on tolls exacted from ships passing through the straits. The legend of a great and wealthy city, whose treasures drew a thousand Greek ships to besiege it, has its roots in this real geographical advantage.

The Troad — the surrounding region — is a landscape of rivers, plains, and mountains. Two rivers, the Scamander (now the Kara Menderes) and the Simois (now the Dumrek Su), flow down from the Ida Mountains to the south and meet in the plain before the city, then empty into the Hellespont. It is on this plain — the "plain of Troy" — that Homer sets the battles of the Iliad, and it is between these two rivers that the Achaean encampment and the Trojan walls stand. Mount Ida, the great mountain of the Troad, rises in the distance, its peaks the home of Zeus and the place from which the gods watch the war unfold.

## The Walls of Priam

In Homer's imagination, Troy is a city of immense walls — walls so great that they were said to have been built by Poseidon and Apollo themselves, when the gods were forced to serve Laomedon, Priam's father, for a year as punishment for a conspiracy against Zeus. Laomedon cheated the gods of their wages, and Poseidon in revenge sent a sea-monster to ravage the land, setting in motion the chain of events that would lead to the city's destruction. The walls, then, carry a curse from their very origins: built by gods, paid for in fraud, fated to be the scene of the city's final ruin.

The walls of Troy VI — the archaeological layer that most closely corresponds to Homer's Troy in terms of its wealth and magnificence — were indeed extraordinary. They were built of massive, carefully cut limestone blocks, sloping outward at a steep angle, punctuated by towers and bastions. The main gate, known as the Southwestern Gate, was approached by a carefully designed ramp that forced any attacker to turn their unshielded right side toward the defenders on the wall above. This is real military architecture, designed by Bronze Age engineers — and it is not hard to see how, over centuries of oral storytelling, these actual great walls came to be described as the work of gods.

## The City Within

Inside the walls of Troy stood the citadel: the royal palace of Priam, the sanctuaries of the gods, and the houses of the Trojan nobles. Homer describes Priam's palace as a vast complex of courtyards, chambers, and colonnades — with fifty rooms of polished stone for his fifty sons, and twelve for his twelve daughters. It is a palace of almost unimaginable luxury, filled with treasure, textiles, and gold, the accumulated wealth of generations of Trojan kings.

While the archaeological evidence for the citadel of Troy VI is less well preserved than that of the walls, the palace at Mycenae or the great megaron at Pylos give us a sense of what a Bronze Age royal citadel of this period would have looked like: a central megaron with a throne and a central hearth, surrounded by storage magazines, workshops, and private quarters. Priam's Troy, in the poem, is the mirror image of Agamemnon's Mycenae — equal in wealth, equal in power, the great eastern rival of the Achaean kingdoms.

## Troy in the Odyssey

The Odyssey begins, not at Troy, but after Troy has already fallen. The war is a memory, not a present reality. And yet Troy haunts the entire poem — as the origin of Odysseus's nostos, as the benchmark of heroic glory, and as a ghostly presence in the many stories that the characters tell of its sack.

The fall of Troy is narrated, from different perspectives, at several key moments in the Odyssey. Menelaus, in Book 4, tells Telemachus of the final night of the war — of the horse, of the hiding Greeks, of the screams of the dying, and of how Helen walked around the hollow horse mimicking the voices of the wives of the Greek heroes. Odysseus himself, in Book 8, weeps openly when the Phaeacian bard Demodocus sings of the wooden horse and the sack of Troy. And in the Underworld, in Book 11, Odysseus meets the shades of the heroes of Troy — Agamemnon, Achilles, Ajax — and hears their stories of what came after.

Troy, in the Odyssey, is the point against which all nostos is measured. Every character who returns from Troy does so in the shadow of what happened there — of the glory won, of the friends lost, of the deeds done in the dust of the plain by the Scamander. It is the city that the heroes left behind, and the place that will forever mark their lives. To return home from Troy is to survive; to speak of Troy is to remember; and to remember Troy is to understand that the end of the war was not the end of the story.
