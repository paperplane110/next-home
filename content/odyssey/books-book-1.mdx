## The Opening Invocation

Book 1 opens with one of the most famous invocations in Western literature. The poet calls upon the Muse — the goddess of memory and song — to help him sing the story of *anēr*, the man, the man of many ways:

> Sing to me of the man, Muse, the man of twists and turns <br /> 
driven time and again off course, once he had plundered <br /> the hallowed heights of Troy.

The invocation establishes the poem's central preoccupations at a stroke: the *man* (not the hero, not the king — just the man, stripped to his essence); *twists and turns* — polytropos, the defining epithet of Odysseus; the long *driven* wandering, the fruitless deviation from the path home; and Troy, the distant horizon of memory that the entire poem circles back toward.

Immediately after the invocation, the poet gives us a startlingly compressed account of Odysseus' fate: all his companions are dead, their ships lost to the sea, and he himself is held captive on Calypso's island, far from home. "Only his wife and his son were left," the poet says, "and the suitors in his halls, plotting Telemachus' death." In a single breath, the entire narrative frame of the Odyssey is laid before us.

## The Assembly on Olympus

The scene shifts abruptly from mortal Ithaca to the house of Zeus on Olympus. The gods are in council. Zeus, the father of gods and men, has just recalled to mind the death of Aegisthus, murdered by Orestes for the crime of seducing Agamemnon's wife and killing Agamemnon on his return from Troy. "Alas," Zeus laments, "how the mortals blame the gods! From us, they say, comes all their sorrows. But they themselves, by their own reckless acts, bring pain beyond their share."

This speech is often called the "theodicy of Zeus" — the poem's brief, dense statement of its theological position. The gods are not cruel; the gods do not gratuitously cause suffering. Human suffering comes, in the final analysis, from human recklessness, from the tendency of mortals to overstep the mark and bring catastrophe upon themselves. It is a lesson that will be acted out again and again in the Odyssey, above all in the deaths of Odysseus' men, who bring about their own destruction through greed and disobedience.

Athena seizes the moment. If mortals bring suffering on themselves, then Odysseus — who has done nothing to deserve his fate beyond the blinding of the Cyclops, an act now a decade in the past — has suffered enough. She pleads with Zeus: let him go. Zeus agrees. Poseidon is away among the Ethiopians and will not interfere. The time has come.

## Athena Visits Ithaca Disguised as Mentes

Athena descends from Olympus to Ithaca. She does not appear as herself. She takes the form of Mentes, an old family friend, the Taphian chieftain, a man Odysseus had known before the war. This is the first of Athena's many disguises in the Odyssey, and it establishes a pattern that will hold for the entire poem: the gods do not announce themselves. They come as strangers, as guests, as old friends. The wise man — and the wise reader — will see beneath the surface.

She finds Telemachus sitting among the suitors in the palace, dreaming in his heart of the father he has never known. The scene is one of quiet, unbearable humiliation. The suitors feast on Odysseus' own livestock, drink his wine, sit on his thrones, laugh and jeer, and court his wife in his own house. Telemachus, a boy among men, is powerless to stop them.

Athena-Mentes sits beside him, speaks to him gently, and delivers the poem's first great command: *Grow up*. Call an assembly of the Ithacan people. Denounce the suitors publicly. Then sail to Pylos to see Nestor, and to Sparta to see Menelaus, and ask after your father. If you learn he is alive, wait one more year. If you learn he is dead, come home and give him a proper funeral, and let your mother remarry. The words are brief, direct, and unanswerable. They transform Telemachus in an instant.

## Telemachus Reproaches Antinous and Calls the Assembly

When Athena-Mentes departs, Telemachus is no longer the boy of half an hour before. He approaches the suitors with a new confidence. He singles out Antinous, the chief of the suitors, the proudest and most violent of them, and reproaches him openly. The suitors are astonished. Who is this boy who dares to speak? Where has he learned to talk like this? "Odysseus is not dead," Telemachus tells them, his voice steady. "Somewhere in the wide world he still lives. And if he does not return — then I myself shall be the heir of his house. I shall be master here."

The book closes with Telemachus preparing for the journey and, at night, speaking with Penelope in the upper chamber. Penelope laments the days of her youth, the husband she lost. Telemachus, now the man of the house, gently corrects her: go to your chamber, mother. This is the work of men. The order of the palace has begun — however tentatively, however fragilely — to reassert itself. Telemachus' journey has not yet begun on the sea. But it has already begun in the heart.

## Key Themes of Book 1

Book 1 is the Odyssey in microcosm. It establishes the poem's major characters (even Odysseus, absent though he is, is the unspoken subject of every scene), its central conflicts, its theological framework, and its characteristic narrative movement from weakness and humiliation toward a restoration of order. At its heart is the question that animates every subsequent book: what does it mean to be a man — to be a son, a father, a husband, a king — when the world has been turned upside down, when the very foundations of one's house have been invaded and occupied? The answer Book 1 gives, through Athena's speech and Telemachus' first tentative act of courage, is: to begin. Simply to begin.
